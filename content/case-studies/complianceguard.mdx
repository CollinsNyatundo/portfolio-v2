## Problem

Kenyan banks, SACCOs and payment providers must spot money laundering in fast, high-volume mobile-money traffic, then report it to the Financial Reporting Centre within strict deadlines. Off-the-shelf tools are built for card and wire data. They don't model M-Pesa patterns such as agent cash-outs or split transfers, and they don't follow Kenyan law, POCAMLA, out of the box.

## Constraints

- **The law sets the deadlines.** A suspicious-activity report is due within two days after suspicion arises (POCAMLA section 44(2); Proceeds of Crime and Anti-Money Laundering Regulations 2023, regulation 38). The clock starts at suspicion, not at a hold. Holds, investigations and approvals are separate steps with their own clocks.
- **Rules must stay in charge.** FATF guidance says a machine-learning score can't be the only reason to report someone.
- **Personal data is regulated.** The Kenya Data Protection Act 2019 limits who can see national ID numbers and phone numbers.
- **Every decision must be auditable**, by an examiner who reads the trail months later.

## Approach

Payments arrive from M-Pesa, CSV files or core banking. A normaliser turns each into one canonical shape, and Kafka carries it to detection. There, 35 typology rules check for known laundering patterns, such as structuring a large sum into many small transfers.

Behaviour and velocity profiles in Redis add context, and an Isolation Forest plus a River online model add an anomaly score. A composite scorer fuses the engine scores with reviewed weights. Some outcomes can't be tuned away: a strong sanctions match always escalates, and a politically exposed person is always treated as high risk.

Above the hold threshold, the system creates a hold, opens a case and drafts the report. An analyst investigates, and a second person must approve before anything is filed.

## Evaluation

There's no real-world accuracy figure, and I don't claim one. No institution's transaction data has gone through the system. The rules and scoring are tested against a synthetic M-Pesa-style dataset with all 35 typologies planted in it, which shows the logic works as designed, not how often it would catch real laundering.

## Results

The full analyst journey runs end to end in a one-command demo: a payment comes in, raises an alert and a hold, becomes a case, gets a drafted report and passes four-eyes approval. The sanctions and PEP screening lists were synced from live public sources on 2026-10-20.

## Security

Personal identifiers go into a reversible vault encrypted with AES-GCM. Revealing one needs a stated purpose and leaves an audit record, and keys can be rotated or erased. Logs scrub national IDs, phone numbers and names automatically.

Decisions are written to an append-only audit ledger in which each entry carries the hash of the one before, so a changed record breaks the chain. An API gateway checks a signed token and a role on every call, and rate-limits each client. Production settings fail closed when a required secret is missing.

## What I'd do next

- Bring the stack up on a real Postgres and Docker host, and prove the audit triggers refuse updates and deletes.
- Run a shadow pilot on one institution's historical data, to measure real alert rates and false positives.
- Replace full watchlist downloads with incremental updates.
- Bring the graph engine live, so related accounts count towards risk.
