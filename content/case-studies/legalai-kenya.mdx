## Problem

Kenyan advocates research across Acts, judgments and practice notes that live in different places and different formats. A general chatbot answers confidently even when it has nothing to go on, and in law a fluent wrong citation is worse than no answer. Firms also can't let one firm's matters be visible to another.

## Constraints

- **Wrong is worse than silent.** The system has to say when it doesn't know.
- **Citations must exist.** A cited section has to be one that's actually in the law it searched.
- **Several firms, one database.** Isolation has to hold even if the application code has a bug.
- **A modest corpus.** 543 Acts and 990 recent judgments, cut into fixed chunks at the source.

## Approach

Retrieval pairs open hybrid search with an Act router. Each question is embedded with Voyage, and Postgres returns 50 open candidates from a vector index and rare-term full-text search fused with reciprocal rank fusion. Alongside that query, Postgres shortlists the 60 Acts nearest the question by centroid similarity. A zero-shot LLM router (Claude Haiku 5.5 via OpenRouter, with Gemini, Groq, NVIDIA NIM and Act-centroid fallbacks) picks up to 10 governing titles from the 548 legislation titles in the index, and Postgres pulls the five best passages inside each routed Act.

Voyage reranks the combined pool, reserving up to eight slots for the best passage of each routed Act and capping any single document at two chunks in the remaining slots.

Two checks decide whether to answer at all. The open-search similarity gate and a 0.45 evidence floor decide whether the query has enough grounding, so the router can never rescue an off-topic question. A citation check then flags any section number in the answer that doesn't appear in the retrieved text before the user sees it. Language generation uses Gemini and NVIDIA NIM as fallbacks for each other.

Each firm is a tenant, and Postgres row-level security decides what every query can see. The server keeps two database clients apart: one carries the user's own session so the database enforces their access, and a separate administrative client, never sent to the browser, handles audited operations.

## Evaluation

The test set is 12,945 synthetic court-submission questions whose model answers cite real Acts. They're kept out of the search index entirely, so they can't leak into results. A hit means an Act the answer cites comes back in the top 10.

Router settings (8 reserved slots, 0.20 routed floor, 5 passages per Act and single-list full-question mode) were chosen on a separate dev slice (rows 600 to 899, where full-question hit@10 was 0.957). Neither of the two reported slices (rows 0 to 299 and rows 300 to 599) played any part in tuning.

Because the Civil Procedure Act is cited in 36% of questions and 10 Acts cover most of the rest, returning the same 10 common Acts for every query already scores 0.803 and 0.770 hit@10 without reading the question. The evaluation gate therefore fails unless the engine beats that constant baseline by at least 0.05 and finds an uncommon Act (outside the top 10) for at least half the questions that cite one.

## Results

Open hybrid search with reranking raised hit@10 from 0.025 with the old keyword engine to 0.303 on the first 300 questions (where settings were tuned) and 0.183 on the next 300. Statutory passages rarely match fact-heavy litigation questions chunk for chunk, so candidate recall at 50 rows stalled at 0.447.

Routing through the statute catalogue first fixed that gap. On the two held-out slices, Act routing hit 0.897 and 0.863 hit@10 on the shorter issues-only query (77% and 73% uncommon-Act recall). Sending the full question as an advocate types it, with parties, facts and issues, raised hit@10 to 0.960 on both held-out slices (0.193 and 0.123 hit@1; 0.944 recall@10; MRR 0.398 and 0.335) and found uncommon Acts for 90% of the questions that cite one (79 of 87 and 80 of 89).

To check string-matching blind spots such as the Penal Code and Criminal Procedure Code, which lack the word "Act", two LLM judges (`gpt-6-sol` and `gpt-6-astra`, Cohen's kappa 0.820 and 0.789) relabelled which statutes govern each question. Against the Acts both judges agree govern, full-question hit@10 is 0.983 on both slices (0.933 and 0.926 recall@10), pending blind review by two Kenyan advocates.

The evidence floor is a deliberate trade. At 0.45, about 99% of real questions pass with the full question (1.0% wrongly refused on both held-out slices) while 34 of 40 off-topic or gibberish queries are refused. A 0.35 floor answered 26 of those 40 and was rejected.

## Security

Isolation is enforced by the database, not the interface. A 37-probe attack suite tries cross-tenant reads and writes, role escalation, tampering with the audit and trust records, and abuse of database functions, and it rolls everything back afterwards. All 37 probes pass on staging.

Every request carries a verified session token. Roles gate citation approval, billing and membership. The API has rate limits, a strict content security policy, and an allow-list for its one outbound document fetch.

## What I'd do next

- Score retrieval at provision (section) level rather than only Act level, and complete the two-advocate review of the governing-Act labels.
- Re-chunk the corpus by section from the source XML, which should raise hit@1 and passage precision.
- Move rate limiting to a global edge rule.
- Run the attack suite against production on every release.
