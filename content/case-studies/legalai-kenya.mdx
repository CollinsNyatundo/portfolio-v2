## Problem

Kenyan advocates research across Acts, judgments and practice notes that live in different places and different formats. A general chatbot answers confidently even when it has nothing to go on, and in law a fluent wrong citation is worse than no answer. Firms also can't let one firm's matters be visible to another.

## Constraints

- **Wrong is worse than silent.** The system has to say when it doesn't know.
- **Citations must exist.** A cited section has to be one that's actually in the law it searched.
- **Several firms, one database.** Isolation has to hold even if the application code has a bug.
- **A modest corpus.** 543 Acts and 990 recent judgments, cut into fixed chunks at the source.

## Approach

</Figure>

Retrieval combines meaning and exact wording. Each question is embedded with Voyage, and Postgres returns candidates from both a vector index and full-text search, fused with reciprocal rank fusion.

Search alone kept missing the Act a question turns on, so a second path now names it. Postgres shortlists the 60 Acts nearest the question, and a small language model (Claude Haiku 5.5 via OpenRouter, with other providers as fallback) picks up to 10 of them. It sees only legislation titles, never test questions or answers. The five best passages of each picked Act join the open-search candidates, Voyage reranks the lot, and each picked Act keeps a reserved slot for its best passage. The query is the whole question as an advocate would type it, parties and facts included.

Then two checks decide whether to answer at all. An evidence floor returns nothing when the best match is too weak, and a citation check flags any section the answer mentions that doesn't exist in the corpus. Only then does a language model write the answer, with Gemini and NVIDIA NIM as fallbacks for each other.

Each firm is a tenant, and Postgres row-level security decides what every query can see. The server keeps two database clients apart: one carries the user's own session so the database enforces their access, and a separate administrative client, never sent to the browser, handles audited operations.

## Evaluation

The test set is 12,945 synthetic court-submission questions whose model answers cite real Acts. They're kept out of the search index entirely, so they can't leak into results. A hit means an Act the answer cites comes back in the top 10.

That metric turned out to be easy to game. The Civil Procedure Act is cited in over a third of the questions, so returning the same 10 common Acts for every question, without reading it, scores 0.803 and 0.770 on the two reported slices. The evaluation now prints that baseline and fails unless the engine beats it by 0.05 and also finds the uncommon Acts that a fixed list never can. Router settings were chosen on a separate dev slice, so both reported slices are held out.

## Results

Before Act routing, hybrid search reached hit@10 of 0.303 on the questions I tuned it on and 0.183 on the next 300. Against the constant 10-Act answer, that was worse than not reading the question at all.

Two changes came after that, and they're measured separately. On the same issues-only query the old engine used, Act routing alone lifts hit@10 to 0.897 and 0.863 on the two held-out slices. Sending the full question, parties and facts included, takes both to 0.960 (288 of 300, 95% interval 0.931 to 0.977). That's 0.157 and 0.190 above the constant answer, and the engine finds the uncommon cited Act for 79 of 87 and 80 of 89 of the questions that have one.

The full question raises a fair worry: the router's language model may have seen these judgments in training and could recognise a case by its parties. Masking party names moved the dev-slice score only from 0.860 to 0.843, but that check ran on the issues-only query. It hasn't been repeated on the full question yet, so I can't rule out that some of the last step comes from recall.

The figure is at Act level. It doesn't check the right section, and hit@1 is still between 0.123 and 0.193. Two LLM judges relabelled which Acts actually govern each question; against the labels they agree on, hit@10 is 0.983. Those labels are waiting for advocate review, so 0.960 stays the headline.

The evidence floor is a deliberate trade. At 0.45 it refuses 9 of the 10 off-topic or gibberish queries in the evaluation, and with the full question it wrongly refuses 1.0% of real ones. On the tuning slice, a 0.40 floor kept the same 9 of 10 and gained one real question, too small a difference on ten negatives to justify lowering it.

## Security

Isolation is enforced by the database, not the interface. A 37-probe attack suite tries cross-tenant reads and writes, role escalation, tampering with the audit and trust records, and abuse of database functions, and it rolls everything back afterwards. All 37 probes pass on staging.

Every request carries a verified session token. Roles gate citation approval, billing and membership. The API has rate limits, a strict content security policy, and an allow-list for its one outbound document fetch.

## What I'd do next

- Repeat the party-name masking check on the full question.
- Finish the advocate review of the judged labels, and score whether the right section comes back, not only the right Act.
- Re-chunk the corpus by section from the source XML, which should do more for section-level accuracy than any further tuning.
- Grow the off-topic test set: ten negatives are too few to decide whether the evidence floor can come down.
- Move rate limiting to a global edge rule.
- Run the attack suite against production on every release.
